# A Neutral HTTP Load Generator

## Requirement

`base-benchmark-load` is a fixed instrument for measuring HTTP servers, used the same way against Base, Tokio, and Hyper subjects.

**Independence.** The client must not depend on `base-scheduler`, `base-http`, or any other subject it measures, so that a scheduler or parser bug cannot move the client and the server together. It parses response heads with `httparse` and frames bodies with its own code. Its I/O runs on plain epoll behind the thin `Poller` trait, so that another backend, such as io_uring, can be added without changing the workers. Dependencies stay minimal: `libc`, `httparse`, `serde_json`, and `base-benchmark` without its default runner feature; no async runtime and no scripting.

**Workers.** Each worker is one thread, pinned to its own CPU when CPUs are given, that owns its connections, its share of the schedule, and its statistics. Nothing is shared between workers on the hot path, and all workers start at one shared instant after every connection is established. Workers busy-poll with a zero timeout. The controlling thread may be pinned to a CPU of its own, never one of the workers' CPUs, and pinning it must not change where any worker may run.

**Modes.**

- *Closed loop*: every connection keeps a fixed number of requests outstanding and sends the next as soon as one completes. Its send-to-response time is reported as service time under saturation, never as latency.
- *Open loop*: requests follow an absolute schedule at the offered rate, with request `k` due `floor(k × 10^9 / rate)` nanoseconds after the start. Offsets are computed from ordinals, never accumulated and never reset to an actual send or completion, so the schedule cannot drift. Latency is measured from each request's planned send time, so that a server stall stays visible in every request it delays (no coordinated omission). The cohort is every request planned in the measured window; it is drained after the window and never truncated to improve percentiles.
- Optional pipelining, up to a declared depth, in both modes. HTTP/1.1 keep-alive.

Run lengths, connection counts, and other limits are named options, not fixed constants. Limits that remain, such as the number of reporting intervals and the response framing limits, must be stated with their reason.

**Measurement.**

- Histograms are log-linear with HdrHistogram's bucketing, per worker and per distribution, and merge exactly.
- Every response is validated: status, framing, and the expected body (by default the body of a preflight response), and optionally required headers or the exact bytes of the whole response. Framing follows RFC 9112 section 6.3, and a response it says ought to be handled as an error, such as one with both `Transfer-Encoding` and `Content-Length`, cannot be framed.
- Responses that fail validation are counted as invalid, by reason, separately from successes and from errors, and are excluded from the latency distributions.
- Failed requests are counted by kind (write, read, reset, early close, timeout, parse, a closing response ahead of them, and never sent), next to the successes; the run continues and reconnects. Connection failures are counted as attempts.
- Every request of the cohort has exactly one outcome: valid, invalid, or failed.
- The sample's operations are the measured period's valid responses only, in both modes, as `base-benchmark` requires of a driver, so that throughput and every per-operation metric count successes. Planned, invalid, and failed requests are reported apart. A run without a valid response in the measured period has no operations, and fails without a record.
- Per-connection counters give fairness across connections, and per-worker counters across workers.
- Starved connections are always reported, however healthy the rest of the run looks, including a connection the server served for a while and then stopped serving. A connection is starved when the server answered none of its requests of the measured period (in the closed loop, none completed in the window; in the open loop, none of its requests planned in the window, if it had any), or when it had a *starved span* longer than `--starved-limit` of the window, by default a quarter of it. A starved span is a time during which the connection's slot, across its failures, timeouts, and reconnections, had a request outstanding or due to send (in the closed loop, always in the window; in the open loop, a request whose planned time had come) and the server sent it no response, valid or invalid. It counts from the window's start, and a span open at the window's end runs on through the drain, until a response, until the slot has nothing outstanding or due, or until the drain deadline. No request outstanding at the window's end is dropped silently: the closed loop drains the requests in flight at its end until they have outcomes or the drain deadline passes. A connection whose starved spans exceed the limit only with the drain, which the server answered in the window and left without a response for no longer than the limit in it, is starved only in the drain, and is reported as such. The summary states, before any distribution, how many of the connections were starved, by each criterion and how many only in the drain, the longest starved span, and the longest wait for a response, marked as a lower bound when that request was never answered, or that none were, and, when the client was saturated, that its own delays may have lengthened them. The record has the count, the fraction, the longest starved span, and the longest wait, and `--fail-on starved` exits without a record when any connection was starved in the window. Starvation is the server's doing: it never saturates the client, and the requests a starved connection holds back are attributed by the shortfall's rules, with the starved connections stated beside them. The latency and service time distributions keep only valid responses of the measured period.
- Client saturation is reported per worker as busy time and as schedule lag, the delay from each planned send to the moment the worker reached it whether or not its connection had room, since busy-polling uses all of every worker CPU whatever the load. An open-loop run whose window ends with more of the requests planned in it unsent than it began with did not carry the offered rate in the window, and that shortfall is reported with the side that held the requests back: the client, for requests its workers had not reached on their schedule by the window's end, which saturates the client, and otherwise the requests' connections, still awaiting responses or reconnecting, which is reported apart and never as the client's saturation. Each part is clamped to between zero and the shortfall, so the parts always add up to it, and a window that carried its rate has neither, however far behind its schedule it began. The connections' part is reported as the server holding requests back only when the client is not saturated and no worker reached a request planned in the window later than the schedule lag limit, since a worker that stalled, however briefly, may have read those responses late; otherwise it is reported as the connections holding them back. Either way the report states how many requests the connections may have outstanding and the round trip under which that many carry the offered rate, since too few connections or too little depth for the server's healthy round trip is a limit of the run, not the server's overload. Default limits are justified by measurements on a measuring host, recorded in the repository.

**Output.** One `base-benchmark` process record on stdout, with one sample over the measured window, so the tool can act as the driver role of `base-benchmark` campaigns. Parameters contain everything that changes the work and nothing that varies between equal runs, such as the address, the default `Host` header that names it, or CPU placement. Metric directions rank servers only by properties of the server: its timings (latency, round trip, send delay, and service time), wall time per success, invalid responses, errors, failed connection attempts, starved connections, the longest starved span, and the longest wait for a response are lower-is-better. The client's own diagnostics, such as its CPU time and context switches, dispatch and schedule lag, busy time, the shortfall of sends in the window and its parts, and saturation, are neutral. The record's signature identifies valid responses by status and body digest, or by the digest of the whole response, never by headers, whose case and order legitimately differ between servers.

**Tests.** No test may assert a wall-clock upper bound, or depend on how much happens in a short time. Timing behaviour is tested with explicit times or a controlled clock, or by causality, such as a latency at least as long as a stall the test server imposed. Runs end on deterministic conditions, such as every planned request having an outcome, with timeouts long enough to be only watchdogs unless the test is about them. Coverage must not depend on timing: every region is reached by a deterministic test.

## Rationale

Samuel approved this design on 8 November 2026, from the load generator proposal and the `base-benchmark` roles proposal, in which this program is the driver role. Counting only successes as operations follows the roles requirement that the driver's operation count is its successful operations only ([`base-benchmark` roles, requirement 7](https://github.com/base-rs/base-benchmark/blob/main/design/roles.md)); before it, the open loop counted every planned request, which made time per operation the inverse of the offered rate. The address is left out of parameters because, under roles, it comes from each service's ready line and so differs between every sample. It generalizes the hello benchmark's fixed-rate client, whose absolute schedule, pinned busy-polling epoll workers, drained cohort with exact counts, separate latency distributions, and byte-exact validation are kept. wrk is unmaintained, closed-loop only, and shares one histogram between threads; wrk2 has about 1 ms pacing accuracy, too coarse for the differences these benchmarks measure. A client built on a subject's own scheduler or HTTP parser would confound every comparison between Base and its comparators.

Saturation was first judged by busy time and dispatch lag. An evaluation on the measuring host hana on 9 November 2026 showed that dispatch lag falls as a worker falls behind, since a response read late counts as its connection having had no room, so it could not catch the worst overload, and that a run could fall 10% short of sending its window's planned requests without being flagged; schedule lag and the shortfall replaced it, as the maintainer authorised as bug fixes. The evaluation is recorded in `results/2026-11-09-hana`. An independent review then found that a shortfall the server caused, as in any open-loop run past the server's capacity, flagged the client as saturated, so `--fail-on saturated` discarded measurements of an overloaded server; the shortfall was split by the side that held the requests back, and only the client's part saturates the client. A second review found that the parts, clamped at zero separately, could name the server for a window without a shortfall when the workers began it behind their schedule, and could name the server for a client's brief stall before the window's end or for too little concurrency; the parts were clamped to the shortfall, and the server is named only when no worker was late beyond the schedule lag limit, with the connections' capacity stated.

Starved connections were made a requirement on 9 November 2026, at the maintainer's request. Puma, with more keep-alive connections than threads, accepts every connection but serves one only when a thread frees up, which with keep-alive never happens. wrk abandoned the requests outstanding at the end of a run, so a run that starved half its connections reported good throughput and latency; the maintainer's fork of wrk finished every outstanding request, so they showed a latency at least the run's length, which exposed the problem. Here the closed loop already drained them, but left their service time out of the distribution and their outcomes out of the summary, and a reproduction with half the connections starved reported a healthy service time and nothing about starvation but zeros in the record extension's fairness counters. Starvation need not be a latency figure, but a run must not hide it.

An independent review then found that a connection served for a while and then starved was missed: with the second criterion, a request that waited at least as long as the window, a request's wait ends at `--timeout`, 10 seconds by default, so in a window longer than that no wait could reach it, and a slot that timed out and reconnected over and over counted as answered. Against a server that stopped answering four of eight connections, and their reconnections, a second into the run, a 12-second closed loop reported no starved connections, as did a 6-second one with a 1-second timeout, and an open loop missed starvation over the last 3 seconds of a 6-second window with a 1-second drain. The criterion also flagged a merely overloaded server in the open loop, whose latency includes the backlog behind slow responses. The starved span replaced it: it follows the connection's slot across timeouts and reconnections, and restarts at every response, so a fair server, which answers every connection at least once a round trip however slow it is, has spans near its round trip, under `--timeout` unless requests fail, while a connection the server stops serving has one from its last response to the end of the run. The limit is a fraction of the window rather than `--timeout`, whose default exceeds a typical window: requiring spans longer than both would have missed the review's open-loop case. A quarter of the window is reached by a fair server only if its round trip is a quarter of the window, with at most four responses a connection in it, and a connection starved for longer has lost a quarter of its share of the window; a connection starved at the window's end has a span that runs on into the drain. Any response, valid or invalid, ends a span, as it answers the first criterion: a server that answers wrongly is reported by its invalid responses, not as starving. The review also found a server that serves each connection for ten requests and then waits on the next, blocking on keep-alive connections the client no longer sends on after a closed loop's window, so the requests in flight at the window's end waited until they timed out in the drain. That is the server's behaviour, and is reported, but in the window those connections' spans were within the limit, so starvation only in the drain is stated apart and does not fail a run under `--fail-on starved`. A third review found that its test depended on the host's speed, since the rotation needed 30 round trips in the window, and failed on a loaded host, and that the tests of partial starvation asserted exact counts from points in time, which a stall of the test process broke; the tests were rewritten to depend on events, a server that answers each request when the next arrives and a server that stops answering after a number of responses, and to assert only what those events cause. It also found that "the window measured them fairly" overclaimed for a connection starved from just under the limit before the window's end, and that a limit shorter than the median round trip flags every connection; the reason now says that the spans were within the limit in the window, and states when the limit is shorter than the median round trip. A fourth review found that a stall of the test process across the window's end still broke the exact counts: it lengthened the spans open at the window's end, so a pool's served connections were starved only in the drain, and with the default limit, any stall longer than it in the window starved the relaying server's connections in the window. The pool and relaying tests now use the whole window as the limit, which no span's part in the window can exceed, and assert exact counts only of the connections unanswered and starved in the window, which only a stall covering the whole window, their watchdog, can change; the starved are lower bounds. It also found that an open-loop request planned and never sent waits until the drain deadline, which can be longer than the window, so only the closed loop shows that the span finds starvation where no request waited as long as the window.

## Scope

The library, the `base-benchmark-load` command, its record, and its tests.

## Validation

- Inspect `cargo tree -e normal` for subject crates and an async runtime.
- Unit tests check the schedule's absence of drift and its inverse rounding, exact histogram merging, framing and validation of split, pipelined, chunked, close-delimited, and bodiless responses, error classification, and request timelines with explicit times.
- Integration tests against an in-test server check exact cohort counts across workers, that a server with fewer serving threads than keep-alive connections, which leaves the others unread by construction, has exactly those connections reported as unanswered and as starved in the window, and at least those as starved, in both modes, in the summary, the record, and `--fail-on starved`, and that a server serving every connection has none starved in the window, with the limit the whole window, so that no span's part in the window can exceed it, that a server that stops answering some connections, and their reconnections, after a number of responses has at least those reported as starved, with starved spans in the window longer than the limit, in both modes, though each was answered in the window, and in the closed loop, where each request ends at `--timeout`, though no request waited as long as the window, that a server that answers each request only when the next arrives has at least the connection of the last request, which no arrival answers once the closed loop stops sending, reported as starved, and every starved connection only in the drain, with the limit the whole window, without failing `--fail-on starved`, that a 40 ms stall remains in open-loop latency with and without pipelining, that corrupted, mis-statused, and mis-headered responses are invalid, that each error kind is counted, that runs without a valid response and setup failures fail without a record, that parameters are equal for servers on different ports, and, from each thread's affinity, that a pinned controller leaves unpinned workers where they were.
- Review tests for wall-clock upper bounds and for counts that depend on how much happens in a short time. Run the whole suite in parallel repeatedly on a loaded host, such as alongside `stress --cpu` with one worker per CPU, and expect no failures.
- Compare coverage run in parallel, single-threaded, and on a loaded host: the uncovered regions must be the same.
- Measurements of the tool itself, such as closed-loop throughput against wrk, belong in investigations, judged on an idle host, not in tests.
